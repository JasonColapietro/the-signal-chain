# Lesson 85 — “Free Bird” · Lynyrd Skynyrd

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Lynyrd Skynyrd
- **Song:** “Free Bird”
- **Album:** *(Pronounced 'Lĕh-'nérd 'Skin-'nérd)* (1973)
- **The tone in one sentence:** A singing slide intro that opens into a long three-guitar climb — clean sustain, Southern-rock bite, and harmonized leads built for a band, not a bedroom.
- **Difficulty:** Advanced
- **Key & tuning:** G major / E minor colors; standard tuning for this lesson (E A D G B E)
- **Section:** JOHNNY’S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

Skynyrd’s guitar sound is a group arrangement. On this song, the slide intro and the later three-guitar build are different jobs inside the same story.

- **Guitars:** Gary Rossington is the player most associated with the slide voice, commonly linked to a **Gibson Les Paul**. Allen Collins and the rest of the guitar lineup bring the sharper lead and rhythm layers. Exact track-by-track guitar assignments can get overconfident quickly, so treat the broad roles as safer than rigid claims.
- **Amps:** Period Southern-rock tone points toward loud tube amps with Fender and Marshall family references depending on the player and context. The behavior is what matters: clean sustain for slide, then midrange-forward crunch for the outro.
- **Slide:** Glass or metal both work. Glass tends to soften the front edge; metal adds cut. Use whichever lets you play in tune.
- **Effects:** Reverb is modest. Delay is not the lesson. The sustain comes from volume, touch, and multiple guitars.

Read this beside Lesson 19. “Sweet Home Alabama” teaches the bright conversational side of Skynyrd; “Free Bird” teaches the long-form build and the way multiple guitars can become one arrangement.

## The Tone Recipe

**The record path, cautiously stated:** Les Paul-style slide voice into a clean-to-edge tube amp, then multiple electric guitars with increasing gain and harmonized lead roles as the outro accelerates.

**Affordable path:**

- **Slide guitar:** Any humbucker guitar on the neck or middle position, tone slightly softened.
- **Lead guitar:** Bridge pickup through light-to-medium overdrive.
- **Amp:** Clean Fender-style for the intro; Marshall- or Fender-crunch model for the outro.
- **Pedals:** Compressor in a low range can help slide sustain, but do not squash the attack flat.

**Starting points (all approximate ranges):**

- Slide tone: Gain **3-5**, bass **4-5**, mids **5-7**, treble **5-6**, reverb **2-3**.
- Outro lead: Gain **5-7**, mids **6-8**, bass **3-5**, treble **5-7**.
- Slide pressure: light enough that the slide does not hit the frets.
- Vibrato: slow and even, moving parallel with the fret.
- Pick attack: strong but not frantic; the acceleration is rhythmic, not just volume.

## What’s Going On Musically

The ballad section lives in a **G major** world, with the emotional pull of **E minor** nearby. That relative-major/relative-minor relationship matters: G major and E minor use the same notes, but they feel different depending on where the phrase lands.

The intro’s slide part is mostly about **singing into chord tones**. Slide guitar exposes pitch mercilessly. If you aim between frets, everyone hears it. The trick is to approach the note, settle directly over the fret, and then add vibrato after the pitch is true.

The outro shifts the lesson to endurance and orchestration. The progression keeps moving while the guitars build from melodic phrases to harmonized lines to fast pentatonic runs. A **harmonized lead** is two guitars playing related melodies, often a third apart. The sound is larger than a single solo because the harmony turns a line into a section.

The form is a long ramp. Do not start at the top. The first lead phrase needs somewhere to go.

## The Drills

**Drill A — Slide Pitch First**

Trains landing in tune before adding vibrato.

```
~56 BPM. No vibrato until the pitch is centered.

e|------------------------------|
B|--/10----10~~~~---/12----12~~-|
G|------------------------------|
D|------------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   land   then move  land then move
```

*Builds: slide intonation. Check the fretted note first, then match it with the slide.*

**Drill B — Long Outro Ramp**

Builds endurance without rushing the build.

```
~92 BPM. Play it three times, louder only on the third.

e|------------------------------|
B|------------------8--10--8----|
G|----------7--9/11----------9--|
D|--7--9------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   phrase, repeat, then lift
```

*Builds: pacing. The same line can grow through touch before you add more notes.*

**Drill C — Harmony Lock**

Trains clean two-guitar harmony or double-tracked parts.

```
~104 BPM. Record the lower line, then add the upper.

e|--7--8--10--12--10--8--7------|  upper
B|--8--10-12--13--12--10-8------|  lower
G|------------------------------|
D|------------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   keep both lines equal
```

*Builds: parallel harmony timing. If one line rushes, the whole section feels smaller.*

## Make It Yours

The main lesson is arrangement. Write a solo that has three stages: slide or vocal melody, single-note climb, then harmony. Do not skip the first two stages. The payoff only works if the listener hears the ground rise under it.

For slide, spend more time on intonation than licks. A slow note in tune is more convincing than a fast note that lands between frets. For the outro idea, record one guitar part, then add a harmony a third above or below. That teaches you how a band can make a single melodic idea feel wide.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 0:00:** the slide note settling into pitch before vibrato widens it.
> - **Around 1:00:** clean sustain rather than saturated distortion.
> - **Around 4:45:** the tempo and energy beginning to climb.
> - **Around 6:00:** harmonized guitars turning lead lines into an arrangement.
> - **Throughout the outro:** the discipline of pacing a long solo instead of spending everything early.

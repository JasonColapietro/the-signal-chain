# Lesson 93 — “Hey Jealousy” · Gin Blossoms (Doug Hopkins)

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Gin Blossoms, with Doug Hopkins' songwriting and guitar voice at the core
- **Song:** “Hey Jealousy”
- **Album:** *New Miserable Experience* (1992)
- **The tone in one sentence:** Bright jangle pushed just far enough into crunch that the heartbreak still feels like a bar band trying to stay awake.
- **Difficulty:** Beginner to Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** D major; standard tuning, E A D G B E
- **Section:** JOHNNY’S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

The exact session rig for “Hey Jealousy” is not reliably documented at the level of model numbers and dial settings, and this is one of those records where over-specifying it misses the point. The audible recipe is clear: bright electric guitars, light-to-moderate crunch, and layers that stay open enough for the melody.

- **Guitar:** Hopkins is best approached here through a bright solidbody or semi-hollow voice. A Rickenbacker-style, Tele-style, or clear humbucker guitar all land in the neighborhood if the top end stays articulate.
- **Amp:** A clean-to-crunch amp is the safer target than a heavy distortion sound. The track has grit, but the chords still speak as chords.
- **Pedals:** If a pedal was involved, the useful modern stand-in is a low-gain overdrive or jangly compressor, not a saturated distortion box.
- **Studio:** The record sounds layered and polished, but not glossy. Exact mic choices and overdub counts are not something I would state as fact.

The tone is a compromise: enough edge to make the band feel real, enough clarity that the suspended and open-string colors still ring.

## The Tone Recipe

You can make the sound with almost any guitar if you keep the gain honest.

**Substitutions:**

- **Guitar:** Tele bridge+neck, Strat middle/bridge, Rickenbacker-style jangle, or a semi-hollow with the tone wide open.
- **Amp:** Fender-style clean with an overdrive, Vox-style chime with the gain low, or a modeler set to light crunch.
- **Overdrive:** Transparent drive, Blues Driver-style drive, or a mild amp-crunch model.
- **Compression:** Optional, light, mostly to keep arpeggios even.

**Starting points (knobs out of 10):**

- Pickup: bridge+middle or bridge+neck, tone **8–10**.
- Amp: Gain **3–4**, Bass **4–5**, Mids **5–6**, Treble **6–7**.
- Overdrive: Drive **2–4**, Tone **5–7**, Level at unity or a hair above.
- Reverb: short room or plate **1–2**.
- Pick attack: steady eighths, medium pressure, with the pick angled slightly for chime.

If the open strings blur, lower the gain before you lower the treble.

## What’s Going On Musically

The song’s center is the familiar **D – A – Bm – G** world: I, V, vi, IV in D major. That progression can become bland if you strum it like a campfire pattern. The Gin Blossoms trick is to keep the rhythm guitars moving with small arpeggios, open-string glue, and chord stabs that make the changes feel restless.

The **vi chord** (Bm) is the emotional hinge. In a major key, moving from I to vi gives you sadness without leaving the key. The guitars don’t need to announce that with a big minor flourish. They just let the Bm arrive and keep the right hand turning.

The arrangement also uses classic jangle logic: high strings ring while the bass and lower guitar notes move underneath. That creates the illusion of more harmony than the part is actually playing.

## The Drills

Original exercises in the song’s style.

**Drill A — Arpeggio Clock (~104 BPM)**  
Train even eighth-note picking across open-position chords.

```
e|--2-----0-----2-----3-----|
B|----3-----2-----3-----3---|
G|------2-----2-----4-----0-|
D|--0-----------------------|
A|--------0-----2-----------|
E|--------------------3-----|
   D     A     Bm    G
```

*Builds: right-hand consistency. Make every picked note the same size.*

**Drill B — Jangle-to-Crunch Switch (~96 BPM)**  
Move from picked arpeggios to strummed chords without changing tempo.

```
e|--2-----2-----|--2-2-2-2--3-3-3-3--|
B|----3-----3---|--3-3-3-3--3-3-3-3--|
G|------2-----2-|--4-4-4-4--0-0-0-0--|
D|--0-----------|--4-4-4-4--0-0-0-0--|
A|--------------|--2-2-2-2--2-2-2-2--|
E|--------------|-----------3-3-3-3--|
   picked D       wider Bm to G
```

*Builds: dynamic expansion. Step on more strings, not more gain.*

**Drill C — Suspended Hook Builder (~100 BPM)**  
Practice small suspended movements inside a D-major pop progression.

```
e|--2--3--2--0--|--0--2--0-----|
B|--3--3--3--3--|--2--3--2--3--|
G|--2--2--2--2--|--2--2--2--0--|
D|--0--0--0--0--|-----------0--|
A|--------------|--0--0--0-----|
E|--------------|-----------3--|
   Dsus color      A to G
```

*Builds: adding motion without writing a new part over the singer.*

## Make It Yours

The larger lesson is how to make a four-chord pop song feel lived-in. Use open strings as glue. Pick smaller shapes in the verse. Strum wider in the chorus. Let the gain sit at the edge where a hard hit barks and a soft arpeggio still sounds clean. That is the jangle-crunch line: the guitar is wounded, but it is still articulate.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **0:00–0:15, roughly:** arpeggiated brightness before the full band settles in.
> - **Verse entries:** the guitars keep moving without crowding the vocal line.
> - **Bm moments:** the vi chord darkens the loop without a dramatic key change.
> - **Chorus sections:** more width from strumming, not a huge gain jump.
> - **Overall:** a guitar tone that feels like a club amp turned up just enough.

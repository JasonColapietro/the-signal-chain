## The Rig & Signal Chain

The *evermore* guitar world is more about close detail than big amp identity. Aaron Dessner's production often blends acoustic instruments, muted plucked textures, and processed ambience until the line feels familiar but slightly unreal. For "willow," the public lesson should stay honest: the exact instrument chain is not reliably documented at a fine level, so we teach the behavior.

- **Instrument:** The hook is commonly heard as a **muted plucked guitar-family part**, with acoustic or lightly processed electric qualities depending on how you focus your ear. Do not overstate the exact instrument without session documentation.
- **Processing:** Light compression, close-mic intimacy, and short ambience are the likely practical ingredients. The note attack is clear, but the decay is controlled.
- **Amp/pedals:** If you use electric guitar, keep the amp clean and dry-to-slightly-roomy. No obvious overdrive is needed.
- **Muting:** The most important "effect" is the palm or fretting-hand mute. The note should bloom just enough to identify pitch, then get out of the way.

This is a modern production lesson: a tiny guitar figure can be the hook if the tone is focused and the rhythm is exact.

## The Tone Recipe

**Record-side target:** close, muted, plucked guitar texture with minimal sustain and a soft room around it. Exact hardware is not public enough to name with certainty, so chase the envelope: quick attack, short decay, warm body.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Steel-string acoustic, nylon-string, clean electric, or a lightly muted baritone-style patch if you are arranging. Use whatever gives you a rounded pluck.
- **Pickup/mic:** If acoustic, record close but avoid boom. If electric, use neck or middle pickup and roll the tone down slightly.
- **Compression:** Light-to-medium to keep the figure even.
- **Reverb:** Short room, low mix.
- **EQ:** Trim harsh pick noise and low-end thump.

**Starting ranges:**

- Palm mute: light, just behind the bridge area on acoustic, slightly farther forward on electric if needed.
- Compression: enough to even the pattern, not enough to erase dynamics.
- Reverb: **1-3 out of 10**.
- Electric amp gain: **1-2 out of 10**, clean.
- Pick/fingers: fingers or a soft pick; whichever gives the roundest attack.

If the line rings like folk strumming, mute more. If it sounds like a dead click, mute less.

## What's Going On Musically

"willow" shows how a small repeating figure can become the center of a song. Harmonically, the lesson sits in an **E-minor** world with a descending, circular feeling. The guitar part does not need to spell every chord in full. It outlines the motion with a few carefully placed notes.

The key musical idea is **ostinato** — a repeated figure that runs through a section. An ostinato works when it is simple enough to remember and flexible enough to survive chord changes. Here, the muted pluck gives the track a pulse that is more intimate than a drum loop and more melodic than a click.

Three skills matter:

- **Short decay:** muting the note so the pattern stays clean.
- **Even subdivision:** every pluck lands in the same pocket.
- **Chord implication:** using two or three notes to suggest the harmony instead of strumming the whole chord.

The restraint is the hook. Play too much and the spell breaks.

## The Drills

These are original exercises for muted plucked hooks.

**Drill 1 — Mute Window.** Trains the exact amount of palm pressure.

```
BPM target: 84. Keep every note short but pitched.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|------------------------------------------|
G|--------0-----------0-----------0---------|
D|-----2-----2-----2-----2-----2-----2------|
A|--2-----------2-----------2---------------|
E|------------------------------------------|
   PM lightly, same pressure every note
```

*Builds: decay control. Adjust the palm until the notes have a soft thud with a clear pitch center.*

**Drill 2 — Descending Ostinato.** Trains keeping one figure steady while the bass note moves.

```
BPM target: 78. Do not accent the bass changes.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|------------------------------------------|
G|--------0-----------0-----------0---------|
D|-----2-----2-----0-----0-----0-----0------|
A|--2-----------0---------------------------|
E|-------------------------3----------------|
```

*Builds: chord implication. The pattern stays calm while the harmony moves underneath.*

**Drill 3 — Finger/Pick Balance.** Trains a soft attack that still speaks in time.

```
BPM target: 90. Play once with fingers, once with a soft pick.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|------------------------------------------|
G|--------0-----------0-----------0---------|
D|-----2-----2-----0-----0-----2-----2------|
A|--2-----------0-----------2---------------|
E|------------------------------------------|
```

*Builds: touch comparison. Choose the attack that keeps the hook warm without blurring the rhythm.*

## Make It Yours

Write a two-bar figure with only three or four notes, then mute it until the decay becomes part of the groove. Move one bass note while keeping the top note steady. That is the "willow" lesson: the guitar can carry a modern pop arrangement by being small, repeatable, and emotionally specific. If you are producing at home, do not bury the figure under reverb. Put it close, compress it gently, and let the tiny inconsistencies make it human.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **0:00-0:15:** the plucked figure establishing the song before the arrangement opens.
> - **Verse sections:** short note decay keeping the pattern intimate.
> - **Chorus lift:** the hook still present even as the production grows around it.
> - **Bridge or late section:** small harmonic shifts changing the color without abandoning the figure.
> - **Throughout:** the attack staying soft and close, never turning into full acoustic strumming.

# Lesson 53 — “Alive” · Pearl Jam

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Pearl Jam (Mike McCready)
- **Song:** “Alive”
- **Album:** *Ten* (1991)
- **Section:** THE 90s
- **The tone in one sentence:** A Strat-voiced lead tone carrying Hendrix and Stevie Ray Vaughan vocabulary into a grunge-era anthem, with enough gain to sing and enough snap to stay human.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** A major / A mixolydian rock center; standard tuning, E A D G B E

## The Rig & Signal Chain

Mike McCready's lead identity on this track is often discussed through the lens of classic Strat players, and that is the right family to hear even if every studio detail is not pinned down.

- **Guitar:** A **Fender Stratocaster-style guitar** is the tool most associated with the lead voice here. The neck pickup and neck-plus-middle style sounds give the notes that round, glassy top.
- **Amp:** A **British-voiced or Fender-style amp pushed into singing breakup** is the practical target. Exact *Ten* session amp settings are not reliably documented in a way that helps a player more than broad behavior does.
- **Gain:** The gain sits between blues-rock and early-'90s rock. It sustains bends, but it does not erase the single-coil attack.
- **Effects:** Light ambience and possible studio layering help the track sit wide. Treat exact pedal and mic claims as approximate unless they come from a direct session source.

This lesson is a bridge. If Lessons 28 and 29 gave you the Hendrix/SRV side of the family tree, “Alive” shows that vocabulary walking straight into the 1990s.

## The Tone Recipe

Start with a Strat-style guitar and make it bigger without making it blunt.

**Substitutions:**

- **Guitar:** Strat-style single-coils are ideal. A Tele neck pickup can work. A humbucker guitar should use coil-split or the brightest neck setting available.
- **Amp:** Fender-style edge-of-breakup, Marshall-style crunch with gain kept moderate, or a modeler profile in that range.
- **Drive:** A Tube Screamer-style overdrive or transparent boost can push the amp. Keep the mids alive.
- **Ambience:** A small amount of room or plate reverb. Delay only if it stays tucked behind the phrase.

**Starting points (knobs out of 10):**

- Pickup: **neck** or **neck + middle**.
- Gain: **4–6**, boost on for sustain if needed.
- Bass: **4–6**, mids: **6–8**, treble: **5–7**.
- Guitar tone: **7–10**, depending on how sharp your bridge/neck balance is.
- Overdrive: drive **2–4**, level **5–7**, tone **4–6**.
- Pick attack: medium-hard, with wide vibrato from the wrist.

The target is a note that blooms after the pick. If the attack is all bite and no body, add mids or move to the neck pickup before adding more gain.

## What's Going On Musically

“Alive” is centered around **A**, with the lead language drawing heavily from **A minor pentatonic**, **A blues scale**, and **A mixolydian** color. That blend is why the solo can sound both bluesy and triumphant. The flat 7 gives it rock authority; the major chord underneath keeps it open.

The riff and solo both use familiar classic-rock grammar. You hear **double-stops**, **whole-step bends**, **repeated motifs**, and **call-and-response phrasing**. The grunge-era setting changes the emotional frame, but the vocabulary is older. That is the lesson: lineage does not disappear when a new decade arrives. It gets re-voiced through a different band, different production, and different urgency.

McCready's lead approach also teaches **solo pacing**. The part does not begin at maximum density. It starts with recognizable shapes, then repeats and raises the intensity. A phrase returns with more vibrato, a bend gets held longer, the register rises. That is how a solo builds without needing complicated harmony.

Rhythmically, keep the feel loose enough to breathe. This is not a grid-perfect shred exercise. It is a rock band pushing around a vocal and then letting the guitar take the room.

## The Drills

These are original exercises in the style of the lesson, not transcriptions.

### Drill 1 — Strat Bend And Fall

**BPM target: 72 BPM.** Train a vocal bend followed by a clean descent through the A minor pentatonic box.

```
e|----------------------------|----------------------------|
B|--8b10~~~~--8--5------------|--10b12~~~~--10--8----------|
G|-----------------7--5-------|-------------------9--7-----|
D|----------------------7-----|------------------------10--|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   lower box                   upper reply
```

*Tone: neck pickup, mids present, gain low enough that every pick attack remains visible.*

### Drill 2 — Repeated Motif Builder

**BPM target: 80 BPM.** Repeat a small double-stop idea, then change only the ending.

```
e|--5--5--5-----5--5--5-------|--7--7--7-----7--7--7-------|
B|--5--5--5-----5--5--5-------|--7--7--7-----7--7--7-------|
G|-----------7-----------6----|-----------9-----------7----|
D|----------------------------|----------------------------|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   same call, new landing       same idea, higher
```

*Tone: use a touch less drive than the solo sound so both notes in the double-stop stay clear.*

### Drill 3 — Build The Solo Arc

**BPM target: 76 BPM.** Practice moving from low phrase to high phrase without rushing the story.

```
e|----------------------------|--8--10--12--10--8----------|
B|--5--8--5-------------------|-------------------10b12~~~~|
G|----------7--5--------------|----------------------------|
D|---------------7~~~~--------|----------------------------|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   statement                    lift
```

*Tone: delay optional and very low. Let the first phrase finish before the second one climbs.*

## Make It Yours

The useful thing to steal is **lineage with context**. Take a Hendrix/SRV-style bend, put it over a 1990s rock progression, and change the emotional weight with attack and arrangement. Use a Strat-style neck tone, but do not make it polite. Push the amp just enough that the note sustains, then make the vibrato wide and uneven enough to feel like a person.

For writing, build solos in three passes: state a motif, repeat it with a changed ending, then move higher only when the track needs a lift. That structure will help almost any rock solo sound intentional.

> - **Around 4:15:** bends resolve with blues vocabulary over a broader rock harmony.

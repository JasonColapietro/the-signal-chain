# Lesson 91 — “There Is a Light That Never Goes Out” · The Smiths (Johnny Marr)

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** The Smiths (Johnny Marr)
- **Song:** “There Is a Light That Never Goes Out”
- **Album:** *The Queen Is Dead* (1986)
- **The tone in one sentence:** A layered clean-guitar arrangement where strummed acoustics, bright electrics, and melodic fills support the song so gracefully that the guitar part feels almost invisible.
- **Difficulty:** Beginner to Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** Concert B major / G# minor color; standard tuning, often taught with capo 4 using G-shape logic
- **Section:** JOHNNY'S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

This is not the Smiths guitar part people usually air-guitar. That is why it matters. Marr's job here is arrangement support.

- **Guitars:** Marr is commonly associated with layered acoustics and clean electrics across this period, using whatever served the song rather than one fixed jangle machine. The exact guitar stack for this track is not something I would state at a forensic level.
- **Acoustic layer:** A capoed steel-string approach gives the part lift without needing heavy strumming.
- **Electric layer:** Clean, bright electric fills can add motion around the vocal without becoming lead guitar.
- **Effects:** Light compression, modest reverb, and possibly chorus-like width are useful substitutes, but the record's lesson is layering, not effect depth.

This closes the three-lesson Marr run from Lessons 89-91: jangle as hook, tremolo as hook, and finally arrangement as hook.

## The Tone Recipe

**The record path, cautiously stated:** capo-friendly acoustic/electric layers, clean amplification, restrained effects, and small melodic figures placed around the vocal line.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Acoustic with capo, clean Tele/Strat/Rickenbacker-style electric, or one guitar double-tracked in two registers.
- **Capo:** Capo 4 lets G-family shapes speak in the concert range.
- **Amp:** Clean, bright, low gain.
- **Effects:** Light compression and short reverb.

**Starting points (all approximate ranges):**

- Acoustic strum: light-to-medium, with the pick brushing rather than digging.
- Electric amp gain: **2-4**, clean.
- EQ: Bass **3-5**, mids **4-6**, treble **6-8**.
- Compression: low-to-medium.
- Reverb: **2-3**.
- Pick attack: relaxed, even, and supportive.

## What's Going On Musically

Using capo 4, the song can be thought of in friendly **G-shape** territory while sounding in B. That matters because open-position shapes have a ring that barre chords often lose. The guitar sounds bright without needing to be loud.

The arrangement leans on a familiar pop motion: tonic, dominant, relative minor, and subdominant colors. In G-shape thinking, that means G, D, Em, and C-type movement. In concert pitch, the emotional center shifts upward, but the hand still feels like open folk guitar.

The important concept is **layered function**. One guitar provides pulse. Another adds sparkle. A small melodic fill may answer the vocal. None of those layers needs to be dramatic by itself. Together they make the song feel inevitable.

That is a mature guitar lesson. Sometimes the part is not a riff. Sometimes the part is knowing which layer the song needs and how quietly to play it.

## Signature Moves

These are original arrangement studies in the spirit of the track.

**1. Capoed open-shape pulse**

```

*Caption: Light strumming keeps the song moving without taking over.*

**2. Relative-minor lift**

```
Feel: smooth change, no heavy accent.

e|--0--0--0--0--0--0--0--0-----|

   Em-shape     C-shape

*Caption: The move from relative minor to IV-shape gives the chorus its lift.*

## The Drills

**Drill A — Capo Pulse Control**

Trains light, even strumming without over-accenting.

```
~132 BPM. Brush the strings; do not dig.

e|--3--3--3--3--2--2--2--2-----|
B|--3--3--3--3--3--3--3--3-----|
G|--0--0--0--0--2--2--2--2-----|
D|--0--0--0--0--0--0--0--0-----|
A|--2--2--2--2-----------------|
E|--3--3--3--3-----------------|
   even pulse, low drama
```

*Builds: supportive rhythm. The guitar should feel like fabric, not a lead part.*

**Drill B — Layer the Top Voice**

Builds an electric fill that sits above an acoustic bed.

```
~136 BPM. Play quietly and leave the last note ringing.

e|--3--5--7--5--3--------------|
B|----------------5--3~--------|
G|------------------------------|
D|------------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   top-line answer
```

*Builds: melodic restraint. Record it over a strummed loop and lower the fill until it barely leads.*

**Drill C — Four-Chord Arrangement Map**

Trains moving through the song-style progression with consistent touch.

```
~128 BPM. One bar each, all the same volume.

e|--3-----2-----0-----0--------|
B|--3-----3-----0-----1--------|
G|--0-----2-----0-----0--------|
D|--0-----0-----2-----2--------|
A|--2-----------2-----3--------|
E|--3--------------------------|
   G     D     Em    C
```

*Builds: smooth open-shape movement. The test is whether any chord jumps louder than the others.*

## Make It Yours

Build an arrangement in three passes. First, strum the song quietly with capoed open shapes. Second, add one clean electric fill after every other vocal phrase, not every phrase. Third, remove any fill that feels proud of itself. That last step is the Marr move.

This lesson is useful because it treats guitar as arrangement glue. If a song already has a strong melody, your best part might be the one a listener feels before they notice it. That is not less guitar. It is better service to the record.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 0:00:** the guitar supporting the song without announcing a flashy riff.
> - **Around 0:30:** open-shape brightness that feels capoed and lifted.
> - **Around 1:00:** small melodic details tucked around the vocal.
> - **Around 1:45:** layers working together instead of one dominant guitar voice.
> - **Throughout:** arrangement restraint as the actual guitar part.

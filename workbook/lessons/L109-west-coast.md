# Lesson 109 — "West Coast" · Lana Del Rey

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Lana Del Rey
- **Song:** "West Coast"
- **Album:** *Ultraviolence* (2014)
- **The tone in one sentence:** Halftime tremolo twang — a noir surf guitar pulse slowed down until every chord feels sun-bleached and dangerous.
- **Difficulty:** Beginner to Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** F#-minor / E-major color for this lesson; standard tuning, E A D G B E.
- **Section:** JOHNNY'S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

"West Coast" sits in the Dan Auerbach-produced *Ultraviolence* world, where vintage-flavored guitars, tremolo, and room sound do a lot of the storytelling. Exact guitar, amp, and mic choices for this track are not something to state as fixed fact without session notes, so the honest target is the behavior: twang, pulse, and space.

- **Guitars:** Auerbach is commonly associated with **vintage-style electrics** — Harmony, Silvertone, Gretsch, Gibson, Fender, and similar studio tools all live in his broader orbit. For this lesson, any guitar with a clear bridge or middle pickup can work.
- **Amp:** A **small tube amp or Fender-style clean/edge platform** is the practical family. You want a little hair when you dig in, but not a rock crunch.
- **Tremolo:** The key effect. Amp tremolo, optical tremolo, or a pedal can all work. The rate should lock to the song's slow sway rather than flutter nervously.
- **Reverb:** Spring or plate-style ambience, enough to put the guitar in a room.

This is a tone where the empty space is part of the riff. If you fill every gap, you lose the spell.

## The Tone Recipe

**Record-side target:** vintage-leaning electric guitar with tremolo, reverb, and a little edge. The exact chain is not reliably published, so dial for a slow pulse and a dry-enough attack.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Tele bridge, Strat bridge/middle, Jazzmaster, Gretsch-style, or any bright humbucker with the tone opened up.
- **Amp/model:** Fender clean, small combo, or any amp with built-in tremolo.
- **Tremolo:** Depth medium, rate matched to the groove by ear.
- **Reverb:** Spring or plate, low-to-medium mix.
- **Drive:** Optional light overdrive if your amp is too clean.

**Starting ranges (out of 10):**

- Amp gain: **3-5**, bass **4-5**, mids **5-6**, treble **5-7**.
- Tremolo depth: **4-6**, rate **slow-to-medium**.
- Reverb: **2-4**.
- Drive, if used: gain **1-3**.
- Pick attack: relaxed, slightly behind the beat. Let the tremolo do the pulsing.

Set the tremolo while playing the actual part. A rate that sounds good on one held chord may feel wrong once the groove starts.

## What's Going On Musically

"West Coast" uses a minor-key atmosphere and a slow, swaying pulse. The guitar part is less about chord complexity and more about **texture as timekeeping**. Tremolo repeatedly turns the signal up and down, so the guitar seems to breathe with the track.

The harmony lives in a dark pop/minor space, with F#-minor and E-major colors a useful way to map the lesson. The guitar language borrows from surf, spaghetti-western twang, and garage-soul restraint:

- **Low notes and partial chords** instead of full six-string strums.
- **Minor double-stops** that sound cinematic without getting jazzy.
- **Tremolo pulse** acting almost like a second rhythm instrument.
- **Halftime feel** that makes the part feel slower and heavier than the tempo alone suggests.

The most important musical skill is restraint. Play the line, let the tremolo move, then stay out of the way.

## Signature Moves

Short illustrative fragments in the track's tremolo-twang language.

**1. The minor twang pulse**

```
Feel: slow sway, ~96 BPM, felt wide.

e|----------------------------------|
B|--2-----2-----2-------------------|
G|--2-----2-----2-------------------|
D|--4-----4-----4-------------------|
A|----------------------------------|
E|----------------------------------|
   let tremolo pulse between hits
```

*Caption: Do not strum harder to create motion. The tremolo is already moving the note.*

*Caption: A short bass-register phrase can sound enormous when the space around it is quiet.*


*Caption: Slide both notes together and let the tremolo chop the sustain after the attack.*

## The Drills

These are original exercises for tremolo timing and sparse twang.

**Drill 1 — Tremolo Lock.** Trains matching the effect rate to your picking.

```
BPM target: 96. Tremolo on, depth medium.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|--2-----2-----2-----2---------------------|
G|--2-----2-----2-----2---------------------|
D|--4-----4-----4-----4---------------------|
A|------------------------------------------|
E|------------------------------------------|
   1     2     3     4
```

*Builds: pulse awareness. Adjust the rate until the volume swell feels connected to the beat, not pasted on top.*

**Drill 2 — Sparse Low-Line Control.** Trains leaving space after low notes.

```
BPM target: 84. Count the rests.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|------------------------------------------|
G|------------------------------------------|
D|----------2------------------2------------|
A|--4---4------4--2----4---4------4--2------|
E|------------------2-------------------2~--|
```

*Builds: restraint. The rests are not empty; they let the tremolo and reverb finish the phrase.*

**Drill 3 — Sliding Double-Stops.** Trains the cinematic two-note movement.

```
BPM target: 72. Slow slides, no rush.

e|------------------------------------------|
B|--2/5--5--5\2-----2/7--7--7\5------------|
G|--2/4--4--4\2-----2/6--6--6\4------------|
D|------------------------------------------|
A|------------------------------------------|
E|------------------------------------------|
```

*Builds: smooth intonation. Keep both fingers moving as one shape so the slide does not wobble.*

## Make It Yours

Put tremolo on a clean guitar and write a part with only three events per bar. That limitation will teach you what this song already knows: the pulse of the effect can carry the emotion if the guitarist leaves room for it. Try the same riff with tremolo off and notice how plain it feels; then turn it back on and resist adding notes. For darker color, use minor double-stops and low-string answers. For more surf, brighten the pickup and add a little spring reverb. Either way, let the guitar move slowly.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **0:00-0:20:** tremolo setting the mood before any busy guitar part appears.
> - **Verse sections:** sparse guitar gestures leaving room for the vocal and rhythm section.
> - **Tempo-feel shifts:** the track feeling like it drops into a heavier sway.
> - **Guitar fills:** small slides and low notes sounding cinematic because they are not crowded.
> - **Throughout:** reverb and tremolo supporting the note, not hiding the pick attack.

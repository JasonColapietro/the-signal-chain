# Lesson 70 — “Do I Wanna Know?” · Arctic Monkeys

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Arctic Monkeys (Alex Turner and Jamie Cook)
- **Song:** “Do I Wanna Know?”
- **Album:** *AM* (2013)
- **Section:** THE 2010s
- **The tone in one sentence:** A low-tuned, fuzz-edged guitar line that moves slowly enough to feel inevitable and dark enough to define a decade of rock minimalism.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** G minor-centered rock; commonly played in D standard tuning, D G C F A D

## The Rig & Signal Chain

The guitar sound on “Do I Wanna Know?” is not fast, flashy, or especially crowded. It is heavy because the line is low, deliberate, and placed with care.

- **Guitars:** Alex Turner and Jamie Cook are associated with several guitars across this period, including **Jazzmaster, hollowbody, and humbucker-equipped instruments** depending on the live or studio context. The exact tracked guitar should be treated cautiously.
- **Tuning:** **D standard** is commonly used for the part, giving the riff a lower center without needing a baritone guitar.
- **Gain:** A **fuzz-edged overdrive** or driven amp sound is the behavior to chase. It is not extreme metal gain; the note envelope stays readable.
- **Amp:** Vox, Orange, Fender, and other British/American amp references appear around the band’s wider gear story. For this lesson, aim for a dark, mid-forward amp with enough top to keep the pick attack.
- **Effects:** Subtle ambience and production space may be present, but the riff’s identity is low tuning, controlled drive, and slow phrasing.
- **Mic/room:** Exact studio mic details are not reliably needed. The mix impression is close, thick, and intentionally shadowed.

The defining move is patience. The riff sounds dangerous because it refuses to hurry.

## The Tone Recipe

You need low tuning, moderate dirt, and tight note endings.

**Substitutions:**

- **Guitar:** Any guitar that intonates in D standard. Humbuckers make it easier to get the darker push, but P-90s or a Jazzmaster-style guitar can work with the amp voiced right.
- **Drive:** Fuzz, overdrive, or a dirty amp with the gain kept moderate.
- **Amp:** A mid-forward British-style model, a darker Fender-style amp with a drive pedal, or a clean amp plus fuzz.
- **Strings:** Slightly heavier strings help if D standard feels loose.

**Starting points (knobs out of 10):**

- Pickup: bridge for bite, neck for a rounder studio-like shadow; try both.
- Drive/Fuzz: Gain 4–6, Tone 4–6, Level 5–7.
- Amp: Gain 3–5, Bass 4–6, Mids 6–8, Treble 4–6.
- Reverb/Delay: low, just enough space to keep the line from feeling dry and small.
- Pick attack: medium-hard, with controlled slides and deliberate vibrato.

If the riff sounds too busy, slow down and shorten the sustains. The menace is in the space after each note.

## What’s Going On Musically

“Do I Wanna Know?” is centered on **G minor** in a low-tuned guitar register. The riff is built from minor-pentatonic and blues-scale gestures: root, flat third, fourth, flat fifth color, and flat seventh. Those are old rock ingredients, but the pace and production make them feel very 2010s.

The line is a study in **rhythmic patience**. Many riffs impress by moving quickly; this one impresses by making every slide and bend feel heavy. The drums leave room for the guitar to speak, and the guitar leaves enough room for the groove to lean.

The riff also uses **vocal phrasing**. It has a question-and-answer contour, almost like a sung line placed on the low strings. That is why it sticks: you can hum the shape even if you do not know the guitar tuning.

Tone-wise, the low tuning and fuzz edge create size, but the note choice creates identity. Do not bury the melody under gain.

## The Drills

Original exercises in the style of the song — low tuning, slow hooks, and controlled bends.

**Drill 1 — Slow Riff Patience**

```
~76 BPM. Leave space after every phrase.

```

*Builds: making a low minor phrase feel intentional at a slow tempo. Tone: moderate fuzz, mids forward, reverb low.*

*Builds: adding blues color without overplaying. Keep the bend narrow and vocal.*

## Make It Yours

The 2010s lesson here is minimalism with weight. Tune down, choose a minor-pentatonic shape, and write a riff slow enough that every slide has a job. Then resist the urge to double the speed or fill the rests. A low, patient line with the right tone can carry more authority than a busier part with twice the notes.

> **Listen For**
>
> - Around 0:00, the **low riff** entering with space around it.
> - Around 0:30, the fuzz edge staying controlled enough that the melody remains clear.
> - Around 1:05, the guitar phrasing like a vocal question and answer.
> - Around 2:10, the low tuning giving weight without becoming modern metal.
> - Around 3:40, the final repetitions proving how much the song trusts one slow idea.

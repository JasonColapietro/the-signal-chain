# Lesson 90 — “How Soon Is Now?” · The Smiths (Johnny Marr)

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** The Smiths (Johnny Marr)
- **Song:** “How Soon Is Now?”
- **Album:** Single, 1985
- **The tone in one sentence:** A pulsing tremolo guitar spread across amps until the rhythm of the effect becomes the riff itself.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** F# minor center; standard tuning (E A D G B E)
- **Section:** JOHNNY'S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

This is one of the great recorded guitar effects, and it is also one of the easiest to flatten if you treat it like a preset.

- **Guitar:** Marr is commonly associated here with a hollow or semi-hollow Gibson-style voice, often cited as an ES-355-type guitar. The exact guitar matters less than the broad behavior: full, ringing chords feeding the tremolo.
- **Amps:** The famous account centers on multiple **Fender Twin Reverb** amps with tremolo, recorded and controlled so their pulses worked together. The exact studio routing is often retold in different levels of detail, so treat precise switch-and-cable claims as approximate.
- **Effect:** Tremolo is volume modulation. It turns the guitar up and down rhythmically. On this track, the modulation is not decoration; it is the engine.
- **Other processing:** Reverb and studio width help, but the pulse is the signature.

This is the second Marr lesson in the run. Lesson 89 is jangle as orchestration; here the orchestration is a synchronized machine around one guitar gesture.

## The Tone Recipe

**The record path, cautiously stated:** full clean electric guitar into Fender-style tremolo amps, likely layered or split so the tremolo pulses create a wide, hypnotic movement.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Semi-hollow, humbucker guitar, or any electric with enough body on the neck or middle position.
- **Amp:** Clean Fender-style model.
- **Tremolo:** Pedal or amp tremolo with depth high enough to make the pulse obvious.
- **Reverb:** Medium spring or plate, but keep the attack clear.

**Starting points (all approximate ranges):**

- Tremolo rate: match the song pulse by ear; start in a medium range and adjust until the throb sits with the beat.
- Tremolo depth: **6-9**, deep but not disappearing completely unless you want the hard chop.
- Amp gain: **2-4**, clean.
- EQ: Bass **4-6**, mids **5-7**, treble **5-7**.
- Reverb: **2-4**.
- Pick attack: steady chord hits. Let the tremolo provide motion after the strike.

## What's Going On Musically

The harmony is sparse. The track lives around an **F# minor** center, but the emotional identity comes from the pulsing guitar bed rather than a busy progression.

A normal riff uses notes and rhythm. This riff uses **amplitude rhythm**: the note rings, and the tremolo imposes a repeating volume pattern. That means your time has to be solid before the effect starts. If you strike the chord late, the tremolo will not fix it.

The other key idea is **sustain through motion**. A held chord might get boring if it simply rang. The tremolo keeps refreshing it, almost like a bow moving across a string. Your fretting hand should keep the chord stable while your picking hand resists overplaying.

For practice, separate three layers: the chord, the tremolo rate, and the band tempo. Only when those agree does the part feel inevitable.

## The Drills

**Drill A — Rate Lock**

Trains matching tremolo speed to tempo.

```
~95 BPM. Strum once per bar; adjust tremolo until pulses feel even.

e|--2-----------2--------------|
B|--2-----------2--------------|
G|--2-----------2--------------|
D|--4-----------4--------------|
A|--4-----------4--------------|
E|--2-----------2--------------|
   bar 1        bar 2
```

*Builds: listening to the effect as a timekeeper. Do not add strums until the pulse feels locked.*

**Drill B — Depth Control**

Trains using tremolo depth as arrangement, not novelty.

```
~88 BPM. First bar shallow depth, second bar deep depth.

e|--2-----------2--------------|
B|--2-----------2--------------|
G|--2-----------2--------------|
D|--4-----------4--------------|
A|--4-----------4--------------|
E|--2-----------2--------------|
   shallow      deep
```

*Builds: hearing how the same chord changes character as the volume dip gets stronger.*

**Drill C — Pulse-Friendly Lead**

Trains sparse lead phrasing over a moving tremolo bed.

```
~95 BPM. Leave at least one beat between phrases.

e|------------------------------|
B|--2/5~----------7--5--2~------|
G|------------------------------|
D|------------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   phrase        wait  answer
```

*Builds: playing with an effect that already creates motion. Space keeps the line from fighting the pulse.*

## Make It Yours

Set a tremolo pedal deep enough that it almost feels too obvious, then write a part with only two chords. Your job is to make the rate, depth, and chord length feel like one instrument. If the part gets boring, change the tremolo depth before you add a riff.

The larger lesson is that an effect can be the composition. Tremolo is often treated as a decoration at the end of a chain. Here it is closer to the drummer. Respect it that way and the guitar part gets larger by doing less.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 0:00:** the tremolo pulse establishing the identity before busy guitar playing appears.
> - **Around 0:30:** the chord sustaining while the volume modulation creates rhythm.
> - **Around 1:10:** width that feels like multiple amps or layers rather than a small pedal in mono.
> - **Around 2:00:** sparse guitar movement because the effect already supplies motion.
> - **Throughout:** tremolo rate and band tempo behaving like one machine.

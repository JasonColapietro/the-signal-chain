# Lesson 92 — “Graceland” · Paul Simon (Ray Phiri)

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Paul Simon, with Ray Phiri's guitar voice at the center of the arrangement
- **Song:** “Graceland”
- **Album:** *Graceland* (1986)
- **The tone in one sentence:** Bright, dry, dancing clean electric guitar that turns township-jive chime into a pop hook without ever stepping on the vocal.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** E major; standard tuning, E A D G B E
- **Section:** JOHNNY'S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

The important name here is **Ray Phiri**, the South African guitarist whose clean, interlocking lines give the track much of its lift. The exact studio chain is not pinned down in a way I would trust at the knob or microphone level, so I would rather describe the behavior honestly than invent a museum plaque.

- **Guitar:** A bright, clean electric guitar is the sound to chase. Phiri is commonly associated with crisp single-note and double-stop lines rather than a saturated rock rhythm tone. Whether the session instrument was a specific model is less important than the fast attack, clear top end, and low-noise sustain.
- **Amp/desk:** The track sounds like a clean amp or very clean recorded chain, kept dry enough that the rhythm sits forward. Any exact amp model you see for this song should be treated as approximate unless it comes from a session source.
- **Effects:** Little obvious gain. If there is compression, it behaves like leveling rather than an audible squash. The reverb is modest; the chime comes from touch and arrangement more than from a wet effect.
- **Band context:** The guitar is part of a lattice. Bass, drums, accordion, and backing voices all leave little windows for the guitar to answer. The guitar tone works because it is lean.

This is a good reminder that "clean" does not mean plain. Clean tone exposes rhythm, muting, and note length. On this record, those are the instrument.

## The Tone Recipe

You can get close with common gear if you keep the signal light and the hands precise.

**Substitutions:**

- **Guitar:** A Strat-style, Tele-style, or any bright semi-hollow. Use bridge+middle or middle pickup if you have it; use a bridge humbucker only if you can keep it clear.
- **Amp:** A clean Fender-style, JC-style, or any modeler clean patch with headroom. Avoid British crunch for the main part.
- **Compression:** Optional, light. Use it to even the peaks, not to make the guitar pump.
- **Reverb:** Small room or short plate, barely present.

**Starting points (knobs out of 10):**

- Pickup: bridge+middle or middle, tone around **7–9**.
- Amp: clean, Bass **4–5**, Mids **5–6**, Treble **6–7**, Reverb **1–2**.
- Compressor: low sustain, level matched, only a few dB of squeeze.
- Pick attack: light-to-medium, close to the bridge for snap.
- Left hand: release pressure immediately after each stab. The rests are part of the groove.

The trap is adding gain because the part feels exposed. Don't. Make the part smaller and more rhythmic instead.

## What's Going On Musically

"Graceland" sits in an E-major world, but the guitar part is not just cowboy chords in E. It borrows from the South African **township jive** vocabulary: high-register double-stops, major-pentatonic melody, clipped upbeats, and little answering phrases that dance around the vocal.

The scale color is mostly **E major pentatonic**: E, F#, G#, B, C#. That scale avoids the half-step rubs of the full major scale, which is why the lines feel open and bright. Add the 6th and 3rd as double-stops and you get the sweet, chiming sound that keeps the track moving without becoming busy.

The rhythm is the lesson. The guitar rarely sits squarely on every downbeat. It pushes into the gaps, answers the bass, and then gets out. Think of the part as **melodic percussion**: short notes, clean releases, and syncopation that makes the band lean forward.

## The Drills

Original exercises in the style of the song.

**Drill A — Upbeat Sixth Chime (~104 BPM)**  
Train the right hand to hit short double-stops on the offbeats without dragging.

```
e|------7-------9---|------7-------9----|
B|------9-------10--|------7-------9----|
G|------------------|-------------------|
D|------------------|-------------------|
A|------------------|-------------------|
E|--x-x-----x-x-----|--x-x-----x-x------|
    1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &    1 & 2 & 3 & 4 &
```

*Tone: clean, bright, barely compressed. The muted low-string scratches should be felt more than heard.*

**Drill B — Release-Control Skank (~96 BPM)**  
Build the left-hand muting that keeps clean rhythm from smearing.

```
e|--7-x-7-x-9-x-7-x-|--7-x-7-x-9-x-7-x--|
B|--9-x-9-x-10x-9-x-|--7-x-7-x-9-x-7-x--|
G|--9-x-9-x-9-x-9-x-|--8-x-8-x-9-x-8-x--|
D|------------------|--------------------|
A|------------------|--------------------|
E|------------------|--------------------|
   press release       press release
```

*Builds: fretting-hand damping. Press only for the chord, then let the fingers relax without leaving the strings.*

**Drill C — Pentatonic Conversation (~100 BPM)**  
Practice answering yourself with short, major-pentatonic phrases.

```
e|------------------|------------------7-|
B|--------7--9--7---|--------7--9--10----|
G|--6--9----------9-|--6--9--------------|
D|------------------|--------------------|
A|------------------|--------------------|
E|------------------|--------------------|
   call               answer
```

*Builds: melodic restraint. Two bars, two ideas, no filler.*

## Make It Yours

The portable lesson is that a clean guitar can be rhythm, harmony, and hook at the same time if you leave enough air around it. Take any E-major progression and forbid yourself from strumming full open chords. Play only two-note shapes on the top strings. Move them on the offbeats. Answer the vocal instead of competing with it. That is how this kind of guitar part earns its place: it does less on paper and more in the groove.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **0:00–0:20, roughly:** the guitar entering as a bright counter-rhythm rather than a wall of chords.
> - **Verse sections:** short note lengths; the clean tone stays dry enough that every release matters.
> - **Chorus lift:** major-pentatonic sweetness without blues bends or rock vibrato.
> - **Band pocket:** guitar and bass interlocking, with neither one needing to fill every beat.
> - **Overall:** the way the guitar sounds joyful because it is disciplined.

# Lesson 89 — “This Charming Man” · The Smiths (Johnny Marr)

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** The Smiths (Johnny Marr)
- **Song:** “This Charming Man”
- **Album:** Single, 1983
- **The tone in one sentence:** Johnny Marr’s jangle as orchestration — bright, layered, capo-friendly guitar figures that turn arpeggios into a whole arrangement.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** Concert F# major color; standard tuning, often approached with capo 2 and E-shape thinking
- **Section:** JOHNNY’S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

Smiths guitar lore often gets flattened into one word: jangle. This track is more specific than that.

- **Guitar:** Marr has commonly been associated with a **Fender Telecaster** for this track, even though many listeners assume Rickenbacker whenever they hear Smiths jangle. Treat the exact guitar claim as commonly cited rather than the whole explanation.
- **Amp:** Clean, bright Fender-style amplification is the behavior to chase. A Roland JC-style clean can also teach the right clarity, though I would not claim it as the definitive chain here.
- **Layering:** The sound is arranged in parts. Multiple clean guitars, or one guitar doubled carefully, can create the shimmer.
- **Effects:** Compression and light chorus can help, but too much effect blurs the right hand.

This begins a three-lesson Marr run. Lesson 90 turns tremolo into the hook; Lesson 91 shows how a layered arrangement can become the guitar part even when no one riff dominates.

## The Tone Recipe

**The record path, cautiously stated:** bright clean electric, commonly linked to Telecaster-like attack, layered through clean amplification with enough compression to keep fast arpeggios even.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Tele, Strat, Rickenbacker-style, or any bright electric with the tone open.
- **Capo:** Capo 2 is a practical way to get the springy open-shape feel.
- **Amp:** Clean Fender- or JC-style setting.
- **Effects:** Light compressor, optional very light chorus, little reverb.

**Starting points (all approximate ranges):**

- Compression: low-to-medium, just evening the attack.
- Amp EQ: Bass **3-5**, mids **4-6**, treble **6-8**.
- Guitar tone: **8-10** unless the top gets brittle.
- Chorus: **0-2** depth if used.
- Pick attack: light, precise, and close enough to the bridge for definition.

## What’s Going On Musically

The song is a masterclass in **arpeggiated harmony**. Instead of strumming block chords, Marr breaks shapes into fast melodic patterns. That lets the guitar act as rhythm, hook, and countermelody at once.

The harmony has a bright major-key surface, but the voicings avoid plain cowboy-chord obviousness. Added 6ths, major 7ths, and open-string-style ringing tones create a lifted color. A **major 7th** is the note one half-step below the root; it adds polish and tension without sounding bluesy.

The capo idea matters because open shapes sound different from barre chords. A fretted shape with open-string logic has more bounce. Even if you play without a capo, think like a capoed player: small shapes, ringing top strings, and fast right-hand accuracy.

The arrangement is also rhythmic. The guitar part dances around the beat rather than hammering it. Keep the hand light enough that the bass and drums carry the floor.

## The Drills

**Drill A — Clean Sixteenth Jangle**

Trains fast arpeggios with even volume.

```
~96 BPM. Alternate pick lightly; no note should jump out.

e|--------0---------0-----------|
B|------0---0-----2---2---------|
G|----1-------1-2-------2-------|
D|------------------------------|
A|------------------------------|
E|--0---------------------------|
   even, bright, relaxed
```

*Builds: right-hand accuracy under a clean tone. Distortion will not hide anything here.*

**Drill B — Add-One-Note Harmony**

Builds Marr-style color by changing one voice inside a shape.

```
~104 BPM. Let the top note ring through all changes.

e|--0-----0-----0-----0---------|
B|--0-----2-----4-----2---------|
G|--1-----1-----1-----1---------|
D|--2-----2-----2-----2---------|
A|------------------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   one voice moves
```

*Builds: arrangement through small movement. Keep your fretting hand relaxed so the ringing note survives.*

**Drill C — Two-Guitar Jangle Stack**

Trains layering without frequency clutter.

```
~100 BPM. Record the lower part, then add the top answer.

e|--0---0---0---0---|--7--6--4--------|
B|--0---2---4---2---|-----------7--5--|
G|--1---1---1---1---|-----------------|
D|------------------|-----------------|
A|------------------|-----------------|
E|------------------|-----------------|
   part 1             part 2
```

*Builds: arranging a clean guitar pair. Part 2 should be quieter than Part 1.*

## Make It Yours

Take any plain major chord and add one moving note on top. Then arpeggiate it instead of strumming. That is the simplest door into this vocabulary. The goal is not to play more chords; it is to make one chord show different faces.

For a band arrangement, keep one guitar as the motor and another as the sparkle. If every layer is bright and busy, the track gets smaller. Marr’s lesson is that jangle is not a tone setting. It is composition.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 0:00:** the guitar arriving as hook and rhythm at the same time.
> - **Around 0:20:** bright clean attack without heavy distortion or long ambience.
> - **Around 0:50:** layered answers that make the part feel wider than one guitar.
> - **Around 1:25:** added-note colors keeping the harmony light.
> - **Throughout:** right-hand precision that still feels buoyant, not stiff.

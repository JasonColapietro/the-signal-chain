# Lesson 51 — “November Rain” · Guns N' Roses

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Guns N' Roses (Slash)
- **Song:** “November Rain”
- **Album:** *Use Your Illusion I* (1991)
- **Section:** THE 90s
- **The tone in one sentence:** A thick Les Paul voice through a loud Marshall-style stack, shaped into long, vocal bends that make the guitar feel like the last grand MTV guitar-hero narrator.
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** B major for the studio recording; standard tuning, E A D G B E

## The Rig & Signal Chain

Slash’s broad recipe is one of the most documented rigs in modern hard rock, but the exact studio chain for any one overdub should still be treated carefully.

- **Guitar:** A **Les Paul-style guitar with humbuckers** is the tool most associated with this sound. Slash’s late-'80s and early-'90s recorded tones are commonly tied to Les Pauls and Les Paul-style instruments, with the **neck pickup** doing much of the singing lead work and the bridge pickup adding bite when the part needs to cut.
- **Amp:** A **Marshall-style high-gain head and 4x12 cabinet** is the core behavior to chase. Silver Jubilee and hot-rodded Marshall family tones are commonly cited around this era, but exact per-song settings are not reliably documented at the knob level.
- **Gain:** The gain is thick, but not modern-metal saturated. The important behavior is midrange sustain, pick definition, and a top end that stays vocal instead of fizzy.
- **Effects:** A touch of studio ambience, delay, and reverb are part of the presentation. Treat any precise delay-time or rack-unit claim as approximate unless it comes from a direct session source.

The honest version is simple: humbuckers into a loud Marshall-style platform, enough gain to hold bends, and enough midrange that the note sounds like a voice instead of a saw.

## The Tone Recipe

You can get close with common gear if you aim for sustain and midrange before sheer distortion.

**Substitutions:**

- **Guitar:** Any Les Paul, SG, PRS, or humbucker guitar. Use the **neck pickup** for the main vocal lead tone; use the bridge only when the part needs more edge.
- **Amp:** A Marshall-style amp, a British-voiced modeler, or a clean amp with a Marshall-in-a-box pedal.
- **Drive:** If the amp will not sustain on its own, use a medium-gain overdrive or distortion that preserves mids. Avoid a scooped modern distortion for this lesson.
- **Ambience:** Light delay and plate or hall reverb. Keep both behind the note.

**Starting points (knobs out of 10):**

- Amp or drive gain: **5–7**, enough sustain without losing note edges.
- Bass: **4–6**, mids: **6–8**, treble/presence: **5–7**.
- Guitar volume: **8–10** for leads; roll back to **6–7** if the front end gets grainy.
- Guitar tone: **6–8** on the neck pickup so the top stays round.
- Delay: low mix, **one or two repeats**, set by ear so it blooms after the bend.
- Reverb: **2–4**, enough space to place the solo in a wide room.

Pick closer to the neck than you might expect. The attack should still be clear, but the front of the note wants to be rounded, not spiky.

## What’s Going On Musically

“November Rain” is built like a rock ballad that leaves a wide stage for the lead guitar. The harmony moves through a piano-centered world, but the solo language is classic Slash: minor pentatonic shapes, major-scale color tones, and chord-tone resolutions that make every bend sound aimed.

The key center is **B major**, though the guitar phrases often lean on blues-rock habits rather than strict major-scale runs. That is part of why the solo sits so well over the arrangement. Slash is not simply running a scale; he is singing through the changes. A bend works because it lands on a note the chord already wants to hear.

The most important musical idea is **long-form phrasing**. Many rock solos speak in two-bar bursts. This one feels architectural: a bend calls, a descending line answers, then the next phrase lifts the whole arrangement another step. The rhythm matters as much as the note choice. Several phrases start after the beat, hang in the air, and resolve just late enough to feel human.

There is also a useful lesson in **register**. The solo sits high enough to clear the band, but the tone is fat enough that it never turns thin. That is the Les Paul/Marshall lesson in one sentence: high notes can sound big if the midrange and vibrato are right.

## The Drills

These are original exercises in the style of the lesson, not transcriptions.

### Drill 1 — Sustained Bend Pitch Target

**BPM target: 72 BPM.** Train the ear to hit a whole-step bend cleanly before vibrato enters.

```
e|----------------------------|----------------------------|
B|--10b12----10b12r10--8------|--12b14----12b14r12--10-----|
G|-----------------------9----|------------------------11--|
D|----------------------------|----------------------------|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   bend, wait, release          repeat higher
```

*Tone: neck pickup, gain in the 5–7 range, delay low. The bend should not wobble until you choose to add vibrato.*

### Drill 2 — Ballad Call And Answer

**BPM target: 78 BPM.** Build two-bar phrases that answer themselves instead of filling every space.

```
e|----------------------------|--12--10--------------------|
B|--10~~~~------12b14~~~~-----|---------12--10-------------|
G|----------------------------|---------------11~~~~-------|
D|----------------------------|----------------------------|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   call                         answer
```

*Tone: keep the delay tucked behind the dry note. Count the rests; do not decorate them.*

### Drill 3 — Chord-Tone Landing

**BPM target: 84 BPM.** Practice ending each phrase on a stable target note instead of stopping wherever the fingers run out.

```
e|----------------------------|----------------------------|
B|--7--9--10--9--7------------|--10--12--14--12--10--------|
G|-----------------8~~~~------|---------------------11~~~~-|
D|----------------------------|----------------------------|
A|----------------------------|----------------------------|
E|----------------------------|----------------------------|
   move, then land              climb, then land
```

*Tone: slightly less gain than the solo sound so you can hear whether the last note really resolves.*

## Make It Yours

The transferable move here is not just “play a Les Paul through a Marshall.” It is **making a solo feel composed**. Take a progression you already know and write three phrases: one high bend, one falling answer, and one final phrase that lands on a chord tone. Leave more room than feels comfortable. Then use tone as support: neck pickup for the vocal center, mids high enough to keep the note alive, and delay just low enough that the phrase gets longer without getting cloudy.

If you usually play with bright bridge-pickup attack, this lesson is a good reset. Roll toward the neck pickup, slow the vibrato down, and make the top note carry the emotion instead of the speed of the line.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 4:10:** the lead tone arrives fat and centered, not sharp or scooped.
> - **Around 4:20:** long bends resolve like sung notes rather than scale fragments.
> - **Around 4:45:** the delay and room sit behind the guitar, widening the line without stealing focus.
> - **Around 8:55:** the later solo pushes harder, but the midrange still keeps the note rounded.
> - **Throughout the solos:** vibrato starts after the pitch is reached, which is why the bends feel vocal.

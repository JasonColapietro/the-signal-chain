# Lesson 84 — “Pancho and Lefty” · Townes Van Zandt

## At a Glance

- **Artist:** Townes Van Zandt
- **Song:** “Pancho and Lefty”
- **Album:** *The Late Great Townes Van Zandt* (1972)
- **The tone in one sentence:** Dry, close, fingerpicked acoustic intimacy — a small guitar part that carries the whole room by staying steady and human.
- **Difficulty:** Beginner to Intermediate
- **Key & tuning:** D major for this lesson; standard tuning (E A D G B E)
- **Section:** JOHNNY’S FAVORITES

## The Rig & Signal Chain

This lesson is an acoustic entry, and the first thing to remove is the idea that the guitar needs to be impressive. It needs to be present.

- **Guitar:** Townes is commonly associated with plainspoken steel-string acoustics across his career, including Martin- and Gibson-style instruments in different contexts. The exact guitar on this recording is not a knob-level documented fact I would lean on. The target is a worn-in steel-string voice with a clear midrange.
- **Strings/pick:** Fingers or a very soft pick both work. Fingerstyle gives the bass a rounded thump and the treble a conversational edge.
- **Recording:** Think close acoustic capture, not a glossy modern stereo spread. Finger noise and small timing movement belong here.
- **Effects:** None needed beyond the natural room. If you add reverb, keep it short and low.

The sound is the opposite of a rig demo. The guitar is a witness. It keeps the song moving without stepping in front of the words.

## The Tone Recipe

**The record path, cautiously stated:** steel-string acoustic, close and dry, played with steady thumb-led motion and enough softness that the vocal remains the center.

**Affordable path:**

- **Guitar:** Any playable steel-string acoustic. A smaller body helps if a dreadnought gets boomy.
- **Pickup/amp:** If amplified, use the most natural acoustic setting available and cut harsh piezo treble.
- **Reverb:** Room in a low range, or none.
- **Right hand:** Thumb for the bass, index/middle for the top strings.

**Starting points (all approximate ranges):**

- Thumb volume: medium-low, consistent.
- Treble strings: slightly softer than the bass until a phrase needs emphasis.
- Acoustic EQ if plugged in: Bass **4-5**, mids **5-7**, treble **4-6**.
- Reverb: **0-2**.
- Tempo feel: relaxed, never dragging; the story needs a walking pulse.

## What’s Going On Musically

The song works like a folk ballad: simple chords, strong bass motion, and a melody that carries the drama. In D, the basic world is **I-IV-V**: D, G, and A, with relative-minor colors available when the story darkens.

The guitar job is **alternating-bass fingerpicking**. Your thumb moves between bass notes while the fingers add small chord fragments above it. This lets one guitar suggest both the bass player and the rhythm guitar.

The important musical idea is **continuity**. The picking pattern should be steady enough to hold the song together, but not so rigid that it feels mechanical. Townes’s world lives in that narrow band: time is solid, but the person playing it is visible.

Use chord tones more than licks. If the chord is D, the safest top notes are D, F#, and A. If the chord is G, lean on G, B, and D. Folk guitar often sounds deep because it chooses obvious notes at the right time.

## Signature Moves

These are original figures that teach the accompaniment style.

**1. D alternating-bass frame**

```
Feel: gentle folk pulse, ~82 BPM. Thumb steady.

e|--------2-----------2---------|
B|------3---3-------3---3-------|
G|----2-------2---2-------2-----|
D|--0-----------0---------------|
A|--------------0---------------|
E|------------------------------|
   thumb   fingers  thumb
```

*Caption: Keep the thumb even and let the top strings answer quietly.*

**2. Walk into G**

```
Feel: let the bass lead the chord change.

e|--------2-----------3---------|
B|------3---3-------3---3-------|
G|----2-------2---0-------0-----|
D|--0---------------------------|
A|----------0---2---------------|
E|--------------3---------------|
   D     walk    G
```

*Caption: The bass walk makes a simple change feel like a sentence.*

**3. A soft turnaround**

```
Feel: quiet reset, ~82 BPM.

e|------2-------0-------2-------|
B|----3-------2-------3---3-----|
G|--2-------2-------2-------2---|
D|--0----------------0---------|
A|--------0---------------------|
E|------------------------------|
   D      A       back home
```

*Caption: The turnaround should invite the next line, not announce itself.*

## The Drills

**Drill A — Thumb as the Floor**

Trains bass consistency under a quiet top voice.

```
~76 BPM. Thumb notes all the same volume.

e|--------2-----------2---------|
B|------3---3-------3---3-------|
G|----2-------2---2-------2-----|
D|--0-----------0---------------|
A|--------------0---------------|
E|------------------------------|
   bass top bass top
```

*Builds: independence between thumb and fingers. Record it and listen for bass notes jumping out.*

**Drill B — Storyteller Walkdown**

Builds a simple bass line through D, A, and G shapes.

```
~82 BPM. Let the bass line connect the chords.

e|------2-------0-------3-------|
B|----3-------2-------3---3-----|
G|--2-------2-------0-------0---|
D|--0---------------------------|
A|--------0---------------------|
E|----------------3-------------|
   D      A       G
```

*Builds: chord movement led by the thumb, not by strumming harder.*

**Drill C — Quiet Emphasis**

Trains bringing out one treble note without changing tempo.

```
~84 BPM. Accent only the marked top notes.

e|--------2>----------0>--------|
B|------3---3-------2---2-------|
G|----2-------2---2-------2-----|
D|--0---------------------------|
A|--------------0---------------|
E|------------------------------|
   small lift   small lift
```

*Builds: melody inside accompaniment. The accent should be felt, not shoved.*

## Make It Yours

Take a three-chord song you already know and stop strumming it. Build it from thumb, then add only the top notes that help the melody breathe. If the part sounds too empty, do not add a run first. Try making the bass steadier and the treble softer.

The bigger lesson is narrative humility. Some songs need the guitar to be a landscape, not a lead character. A plain D chord, picked steadily, can hold more weight than a clever substitution if the hand stays honest.

> **Listen For**
>
> - **Around 0:00:** the close, dry acoustic sound and the small finger noise around it.
> - **Around 0:30:** bass movement holding the song together without sounding busy.
> - **Around 1:10:** the way the guitar stays under the vocal instead of answering every line.
> - **Around 2:00:** tiny dynamic lifts in the top strings.
> - **Throughout:** the pulse staying human, not metronomic.
